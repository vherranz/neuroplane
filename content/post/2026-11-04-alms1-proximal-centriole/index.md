We are pleased to share our latest collaborative article, published in the *Journal of Cell Biology*.

Alström syndrome is a rare human ciliopathy caused by mutations in *ALMS1*, yet how this protein contributes to cilia function has long been unclear. Unlike most ciliopathy-associated proteins, which act at the cilium or its base, ALMS1 localizes to the proximal end of centrioles — raising the possibility that it supports cilia through a distinct mechanism.

In this study we find that, without ALMS1, centrioles are short and frequently break at their proximal ends. These truncated, broken centrioles display reduced microtubule-organizing activity and disrupted centriole linkers. As a consequence, Golgi-derived cargos — including transmembrane components of the transition zone — are no longer efficiently trafficked to the centrosome. ALMS1 is therefore required for transition zone assembly, ciliogenesis, and the control of ciliary membrane composition.

In mice, disruption of ALMS1 causes a progressive, age-dependent loss of cilia in tissues affected in Alström syndrome, including the retina, kidney and brain. Together, these findings suggest that Alström syndrome arises from a loss of proximal centriole integrity and centrosome-directed protein trafficking, which compromises both the assembly and the maintenance of cilia.
